The Economic Case for Trade

Comparative Advantage

Every country gains from trade by specializing in what it produces relatively well, even if it produces everything at higher cost than its trading partner.

What is not required

  • An absolute cost advantage in any good
  • The United States gains from trading with China even if U.S. firms could produce every good more cheaply

What is required

  • Different opportunity costs across the same goods
  • Each country specializes in the good for which it gives up less of others
  • Both countries consume beyond what autarky allowed

The Production Possibility Frontier

The PPF shows everything a country could produce, given its labor and capital.

Its slope is the opportunity cost: how much of one good must be given up to make one more of the other.

China Has a Different Frontier

Same logic, different opportunity costs — because China has different factor endowments (more labor, less capital).

Trade Lets Both Consume Beyond Autarky

The two countries agree on a trade price (here 6 shirts per computer).

Each fully specializes. Both consume on a higher indifference curve than autarky allowed.

Reproduces Oatley’s Figure 3.3 in spirit — full specialization with trade. Walk through the numbers: U.S. specializes in computers (produces 100 million), exports most of them, gets shirts in return. China specializes in shirts (produces 400 million), exports most, gets computers in return. At the trade price of 6 shirts per computer, U.S. consumption moves from (60 computers, 120 shirts) under autarky to roughly (75, 150) with trade. China’s consumption moves from (13, 140) to (25, 250). Both countries are unambiguously better off — they consume more of both goods than they could produce on their own. This welfare result is the foundation for the standard economic case against protection.

Where Does Comparative Advantage Come From?

Ricardian model (Ricardo, 1817)

Comparative advantage comes from differences in labor productivity — i.e., technology.

Countries specialize where their technology gives them an edge.

Key insight: technology, not endowments, drives the pattern.

Heckscher-Ohlin (H-O) model (early 20th c.)

Comparative advantage comes from factor endowments — the relative supply of capital, labor, land.

  • Capital-abundant countries → capital-intensive goods (autos, aircraft)
  • Labor-abundant countries → labor-intensive goods (apparel)

Key insight: who owns the abundant factor wins from trade.

Two workhorse models. Ricardo (early 19th c.) said comparative advantage comes from technology — Britain has better looms, so Britain makes cloth more productively than Portugal even if both have the same labor. Heckscher-Ohlin (early 20th c.) said it comes from factor endowments — Britain has more capital, so Britain specializes in capital-intensive goods, while Portugal with more labor specializes in labor-intensive goods. The bar chart below illustrates the H-O intuition with Oatley’s example: in auto manufacturing, labor is only about 25–30% of costs, with capital dominating; in apparel, labor takes the lion’s share. The H-O punchline is the one that matters most for political economy: the owners of the abundant factor gain from trade, while the owners of the scarce factor lose. In the U.S., that means capital-abundant industries (and capital owners) gain from trade; labor-abundant ones (and lower-skilled workers) lose. Hold onto that thought — this distributional asymmetry drives much of the politics of trade. We don’t need the full Stolper-Samuelson theorem today, but the intuition is: trade isn’t neutral within a country, even when it’s net-positive overall.

Trade Policy and Welfare

Standard introductory microeconomics. The left panel shows the welfare consequences of opening a closed economy to trade; the right panel shows how a tariff partially reverses them.

Opening to trade transfers area A from producers to consumers and creates area B as new welfare. A tariff partially reverses this: area A is transferred back to producers as new producer surplus, area C is collected by the government, and areas B and D are destroyed as deadweight loss.

This is the strongest economic argument for trade. Set up the small open economy: world price is below domestic autarky price; the country imports the difference. A tariff raises the domestic price above the world price. Producers respond by producing more (Q1 → Q2). Consumers respond by buying less (Q4 → Q3). Imports shrink. Government collects tariff revenue on what’s still imported (rectangle C). Now the welfare accounting: producers gain area A (their surplus increases because they get a higher price). Government collects area C (tariff revenue). But consumers lose A + B + C + D (their surplus falls by a much larger trapezoid). Net welfare change: gains are A + C; losses are A + B + C + D; net is −B − D. Areas B and D are pure deadweight loss — production we didn’t need at this cost, and consumption that doesn’t happen because the price is artificially high. This is the punchline: protection doesn’t transfer welfare from consumers to producers. It transfers some welfare to producers and government, AND destroys some welfare entirely. That’s why “free trade is good” isn’t just an ideological claim — it’s a welfare arithmetic claim.

What This Theory Assumes

The standard comparative-advantage argument rests on three contested assumptions:

Market clearing

Prices and wages adjust freely; labor markets clear; no persistent unemployment.

Trade adjustment costs in practice can persist for decades and remain geographically concentrated.

Constant or decreasing returns

Markets are competitive rather than oligopolistic.

Trade in aircraft, semiconductors, and software is dominated by a small number of firms operating under increasing returns to scale.

Neutral terms of trade

Relative prices do not systematically favor some producers over others.

The Prebisch-Singer hypothesis argues that commodity exporters face deteriorating terms of trade against manufactured-goods exporters.

The case for state intervention, in the next section, starts from the limits of these assumptions.

This is a signposting slide — flagging that the theory we just walked through makes assumptions that aren’t always true. Three big ones. (1) Market clearing: standard trade theory assumes prices and wages adjust to keep labor employed. In reality, the China Shock literature (Autor-Dorn-Hanson and others) shows that trade adjustment can be slow and geographically concentrated, with displaced workers not finding equivalent employment for years or decades. (2) Constant or decreasing returns to scale: the textbook story assumes competitive markets — many firms, no one with market power. Modern trade in aircraft, semiconductors, pharmaceuticals, software is dominated by a small number of firms with massive economies of scale. That’s “new trade theory” (Krugman and others) — strategic interaction, market power, government intervention can shape who wins. Different politics, different policy implications. (3) Neutral terms of trade: the model assumes the relative prices of traded goods don’t systematically favor some producers. Prebisch and Singer argued in the 1950s that commodity exporters (developing countries) face deteriorating terms of trade against manufactured-goods exporters — a key intellectual foundation of import-substitution strategies in Latin America and elsewhere. The next section takes these up directly, since the case for intervention begins by relaxing them.

Distribution Within Countries

Distributional conflict over trade is both interstate and intrastate. Country-level accounts of “winners” and “losers” obscure the within-country coalitions that produce national trade positions.

Between countries

Some countries gain more from a given agreement than others. Bargaining power determines the distribution.

Within countries

Industries, regions, and factor owners gain and lose unevenly.

U.S. agriculture is heavily protected and subsidized despite farmers comprising roughly 1.5% of the population. The bicameral Senate gives Wyoming the same representation as California, amplifying the political power of rural producers.

“The U.S. position” in any negotiation is the position of the domestic coalition with sufficient political voice to define it.

This slide is critical and worth slowing down for. The standard trade-theory presentation talks about country-level gains and losses, which makes it sound like the political problem is just “U.S. vs. China” or “developed vs. developing.” But that’s only half of it. The other half — and arguably the more important half for politics — is within-country distribution. Even when comparative advantage says the U.S. gains overall, those gains go to some American workers, firms, and regions while other American workers, firms, and regions lose. The political question isn’t just “do we gain on net?” — it’s “which Americans gain, and which lose, and which group has more political power?” Big Ag is the clearest U.S. example. Farmers are tiny — about 1.5% of the population. But agriculture is one of the most heavily protected and most heavily subsidized sectors in the U.S. economy. Why? Because the bicameral system in the U.S. Senate gives each state two senators regardless of population, and farm-state senators wield enormous power over farm-state legislation. Wyoming (population 580,000) gets the same Senate representation as California (population 39 million). That structural feature of American political institutions massively amplifies the political power of rural producer interests. This is the society-centered view of trade politics (Stolper-Samuelson, coalitions, the Ricardo-Viner / specific-factors model), which the next section contrasts with a state-centered view. The point to make here: when we say “the U.S. position” in trade negotiations, we don’t mean some abstract national interest. We mean whichever domestic coalition has the political voice to define the U.S. position. That’s a much messier story than the textbook lets on.

The Case for State Intervention

The State-Centered Approach: Two Core Claims

Society-Centered State-Centered
1 · Protection can RAISE welfare Protection always destroys welfare — it forfeits the gains from trade and traps resources in losing industries. Under specific conditions (scale, learning, rents), targeted intervention can move resources where the market won’t, increasing country-level aggregate welfare.
2 · The state can act ON ITS OWN Policy is the outgrowth of interest-group pressure. The state has no goals of its own. Under specific conditions, insulated policymakers pursue a national objective that no lobby asked for.

Both claims must hold jointly:

  1. If protection cannot raise welfare, intervention is pointless.
  2. If the state cannot act autonomously, the account reduces to society-centered lobbying.

The state-centered approach rests on two assumptions, and each one directly contradicts a pillar of the society-centered model. The first is a claim about welfare. The society-centered approach inherited the standard trade model’s verdict that protection is deadweight loss — it forfeits the gains from trade and keeps resources stuck in comparatively disadvantaged industries. The state-centered approach says: not always. Under a specific and limited set of conditions — economies of scale, learning effects, oligopoly rents — there are industries the market won’t move resources into even though society would gain in the long run, and a well-aimed intervention can correct that. The second assumption is about politics. The society-centered approach treats national policy as the resultant of interest-group forces. The state-centered approach says that under certain institutional conditions, policymakers are insulated enough to pursue an objective of their own — boosting a strategic industry, catching a rival, securing a supply — that no domestic lobby necessarily demanded. The crucial logical point for students: you need both assumptions together. If protection can never raise welfare, then autonomy just lets the state destroy value more efficiently. If the state can never act autonomously, then even a real welfare opportunity gets captured and distorted by whoever lobbies hardest. The rest of the lecture is essentially an examination of when — and whether — these two strong assumptions actually hold.

Revisiting Core Assumptions - Market Failures Everywhere

The gains-from-trade verdict — do not intervene — rests on the three contested assumptions from the previous section. The state-centered approach is favored by those who focus on the limits of those assumptions.

Assumption What the free-trade case assumes How the state-centered approach relaxes it →
Market clearing Factors are allocated to their best use; no lasting unemployment — so a tariff can only destroy value. Scale + learning curves strand factors below their long-run value — a social-vs-private return gap the market won’t close without public guidance.
Constant / decreasing returns Markets are competitive: many firms, no market power. High-tech runs on increasing returns and oligopoly. A few firms earn rents, and the country that hosts them captures the income.
Neutral terms of trade Doesn’t really matter what goods you specialize in. National wealth accrues when countries can move up the value chain. Staying stuck in primary production is bad for wealth and growth.

Relax perfect competition and constant returns and the case for free trade becomes more complicated and less absolute.

This slide connects the two halves of the lecture, and it’s worth making explicit because it shows that the case for intervention is not a separate topic — it is, in effect, the mirror image of the free-trade model from the first section. Go back to the “What This Theory Assumes” slide: it flagged three assumptions sitting behind the clean gains-from-trade result. Take them in order. One, market clearing: the standard model assumes factors glide to their highest-value use, which is precisely why a tariff could only ever block that movement and destroy value. The state-centered approach denies the premise — economies of scale and learning curves can strand resources below their long-run potential, opening a gap between social and private returns that the market won’t close on its own, and that gap is the entire welfare case for intervention we’re about to build. Two, constant or decreasing returns: the standard model assumed competition, many firms, nobody with market power. Drop that assumption and you land in the world of increasing returns and oligopoly, where a handful of firms earn rents and the country hosting them pockets the income. This is the “new trade theory” mentioned on the assumptions slide — Krugman’s world, and the basis of strategic-trade theory. Three, neutral terms of trade: relax the idea that no producer is systematically favored and you see that rents and first-mover advantages are contestable prizes a government can try to seize with a subsidy. The point to carry forward: every tool in the next section — infant-industry protection, subsidies, state-directed credit — depends on relaxing one or more of these three assumptions. That gives students a diagnostic they can use on any real-world intervention: ask which assumption the advocate is claiming fails here. If they can’t name one, the argument is probably interest-group politics described in the vocabulary of the national interest.

Infant Industries and Industrial Policy

The Infant-Industry Case

A new industry may be unprofitable today but efficient tomorrow — if it survives long enough to grow up. Two mechanisms create that gap:

Economies of scale

Big fixed costs (an aircraft program ≈ $3B+). Unit cost falls only at high volume. A new entrant can’t reach that volume against incumbents who already have.

Economies of experience

Efficiency comes from doing — seasoned managers, skilled workers, reliable suppliers. The learning curve accrues through cumulative production; it cannot simply be purchased.

The argument has mercantilist roots: Alexander Hamilton (1791) and Friedrich List both sought tariffs to develop manufacturing against a dominant Britain — and the same argument was later applied to postwar Japan.

The economic core of the case for intervention is the infant-industry argument, and it is worth slowing down because every modern industrial-policy debate is a variation on it. The standard trade model rules out welfare-improving intervention by assumption: factors flow to their highest return, so a tariff only blocks that flow. To justify intervention you must identify something that stops factors from moving into an industry that would, in the long run, pay off. The classic answer is the infant industry — a firm not efficient initially but efficient eventually, if it is given time to mature. Two mechanisms generate that gap. Economies of scale: when fixed costs are enormous — Oatley’s example is a commercial aircraft program costing billions — unit costs fall only at high volume, but a newcomer can’t sell enough high-cost early output against established low-cost rivals to ever reach that volume. Economies of experience, or learning by doing: efficient production needs seasoned managers, skilled workers, and reliable suppliers, none of which exist in an infant industry by definition. Costs fall as you accumulate experience. The political pedigree matters: this is not a left-wing idea or a new idea. Alexander Hamilton made the argument in 1791 to build American manufacturing against British dominance; Friedrich List systematized it in Germany a half-century later; and many credit Japan’s postwar ascent to the same logic. Free-trade Britain was the incumbent; everyone climbing behind it reached for the tariff.

Climbing the Learning Curve

The newcomer’s dilemma: costs fall only with volume, but volume is unattainable while costs remain high. A temporary tariff or subsidy can solve this “low-level equilibrium trap” .

This figure makes the learning curve concrete and gives students something visual to remember. When Airbus assembled its very first fuselage it took roughly 340,000 person-hours. By the 75th aircraft that had fallen to about 85,000, and eventually to around 43,000. That improvement was not purchased; it was achieved through cumulative repetition — workers learning the job, managers refining the process, suppliers becoming reliable. Now see the trap. The newcomer is expensive precisely because it hasn’t yet produced much, and it can’t produce much because, being expensive, it can’t win customers away from incumbents who are already far down their own curve. Who buys the high-cost early output? In an open market, no one — so the firm never reaches the volume that would make it competitive. This is the welfare argument for a temporary intervention: guarantee the infant the domestic market for a while, let it produce enough to slide down the curve, and once it’s genuinely competitive, remove the protection. The phrase to underline is temporary. The entire welfare case depends on the tariff coming off once the industry grows up. Hold that thought, because the most serious real-world criticism of industrial policy is that the protection almost never comes off — the infant becomes a dependent adult with a powerful lobby.

A Tariff Is Rarely the Best Tool

The economists’ rule: target the actual distortion.

  1. A profitable-but-cash-starved firm should borrow — and if financing is the binding constraint, the government can subsidize the borrowing itself (a loan or guarantee), not the output.
  2. A skills-spillover problem needs workforce training, not a tariff.
  3. Intervention is justified only where a real market failure creates a major coordination problem (government needs to make both the demand and supply side of the market).

This slide is Oatley’s “A Closer Look” box, and it teaches students how an economist actually reasons about intervention — by asking what specific market failure you’re correcting and choosing the narrowest tool that fixes it. Start at the left. A tariff helps the infant, but it does so by taxing domestic consumers, who now pay more — a real welfare loss. So move right: a subsidy delivers the same help to the firm without gouging consumers, which is why economists prefer it to a tariff. But now comes the sharper objection, and it applies to subsidies too. If a firm really will be profitable in the long run, it should be able to borrow against those future profits in private capital markets to cover its early losses. If it can borrow, it doesn’t need the government. And if it can’t borrow — if private investors, looking at the same future, decline to fund it — then the market is telling you this isn’t society’s best use of scarce capital, so the government shouldn’t fund it either. That argument is the free-market case against industrial policy in compressed form. It fails in exactly two circumstances, and they’re worth memorizing because they define the legitimate zone for intervention. One: the payoff comes from training workers who can then quit and take their skills to rivals, so no single firm can capture the return — a classic spillover, best fixed by broad training programs, not firm subsidies. Two: the capital market itself is broken and won’t lend to good projects — in which case the first-best fix is to repair the capital market, not to subsidize the firm. The through-line: intervention is defensible only where you can name a real market failure that blocks the private borrowing solution.

State Strength: Who Can Actually Do This?

Industrial policy = tax breaks, subsidies and state credit, protection, and procurement, used to steer resources toward favored industries.

Whether a state can do so depends on state strength — how insulated policymakers are from interest groups.

State Institutions & track record Insulation
Japan — STRONG Centralized authority; MITI/METI + Ministry of Finance coordinate. “Administrative guidance” steered capital from heavy industry (1950s) to high-tech (1970s). High
France — STRONG Insulated bureaucracy; “National Champions” strategy. Backed Renault, steel — and a national computer firm (Machines Bull) that flopped. High
United States — WEAK Federalism + divided powers + porous agencies → captured easily. Yet runs a de facto industrial policy through DEFENSE: R&D, procurement, the internet, early chips. Low (but the Pentagon is a loophole)

Strength cuts both ways. Insulation lets a state plan for the long run and pull protection once the infant matures — but it also removes the check that stops the state from picking badly.

Now we connect the economics to institutions, because even if intervention can work in theory, only some states are equipped to implement it. First, the definition: industrial policy is the use of a broad toolkit — tax policy, subsidies including state-directed credit, traditional protection, and government procurement — to channel resources away from some industries and toward those the state wants to grow. The capacity to wield that toolkit coherently is what Oatley calls state strength: the degree to which policymakers are insulated from interest-group pressure. Strong states centralize authority, coordinate across agencies, and offer few access points to lobbyists. Japan is the textbook strong state — MITI, now METI, working with the Ministry of Finance, used “administrative guidance” to move capital from steel and shipbuilding in the early postwar years into high-technology in the 1970s, and many credit this for the Japanese miracle. France is the other strong-state case, with its insulated bureaucracy and its “National Champions” strategy — though notice Oatley’s honest detail that the French national computer champion, Machines Bull, failed to become competitive, an early reminder that strength guarantees coherence, not success. The United States is the canonical weak state: federalism, separated powers, and porous agencies create so many channels for lobbying that officials struggle to act coherently in any “national interest.” And yet — this is the move I want students to catch — the U.S. runs a large de facto industrial policy through the back door of national defense: Pentagon R&D and procurement underwrote computing, the internet, and the early semiconductor industry. That defense loophole is the historical seed of today’s “economic security” turn. Last point, and it’s the double edge: insulation is exactly what lets a strong state plan long-term and, crucially, withdraw protection once an industry matures — but it’s also what removes the feedback that would tell the state it has chosen badly. Strength is not the same as good judgment.

How to Read Academic Books and Articles

Scholarly books and articles are not textbooks, and they are not written for students. They are written by scholars to transmit scholarly ideas for critique and debate to other scholars. Accordingly, you need to approach graduate reading — and especially book-length scholarship — differently than other forms of writing. This is a skill, rather than a gift, and so it can be learned. This guide provides you with a structure or method for how to approach a variety of types of scholarly material in a way to efficiently and effectively extract meaning from them. Most of the time, faculty expect students to acquire this skill through trial and error, and indeed, to become proficient you need to practice (see Ted and Lizzie, but not Allen). But, hopefully this guide helps to unmask the “hidden curriculum” of graduate study. Many of you did some version of this reading as undergraduates. However, you are now expected to read at much greater volumes and to be able to have a more commanding understanding of the material. That takes some tactical adjustment.

The bottom line up front (the BLUF, in State Department Speak)

A textbook chapter is organized to deliver information, usually communicated as settled fact. A scholarly article is organized to pose a question or puzzle; propose a solution, answer, or conceptual framework for interpreting the question; and to defend this proposition. A scholarly book does the same thing, but at a scale that requires the argument to be built across chapters — because the claim is broad, the mechanism has several moving parts, or the evidence has to be assembled across cases, periods, or countries that no single article could accommodate.

That difference changes how you read scholarly work as opposed to a textbook. You are not trying to absorb everything on the page. You are trying to find four elements:

  1. The question the author is asking
  2. The answer they give (their claim)
  3. The reason the answer is supposed to be true (their mechanism or evidence)
  4. The limits of their claim (does the evidence support it, does the mechanism make sense, are there alternative explanations for the empirical evidence, are there scope conditions that limit how broadly their answer applies, etc.)

For a book, add a fifth:

  1. The target — the dominant explanation, policy consensus, or prior generation of scholarship the book is arguing against

The fifth is the one that does not much matter for articles, where the target is usually stated outright in the second paragraph, and it is the one graduate students most often miss in books. Almost every academic book is written in opposition to an extant narrative or theoretical construct. Until you can name that ‘argument space’, the book appears to mainly be a series of assertions and you will not understand why the author needed three hundred pages to make them.

If you complete a reading with those elements written down in your own words, you have done your job. Everything else in the book or article exists to support them.

The three-pass method

Do not read scholarly work front-to-back like a novel. Read it three times, fast to slow. This takes less total time than one grinding read-through, because by the time you read carefully you already know what matters.

The three passes below are written for an article. The section after this one applies the same three passes to a book, where they get stretched across several weeks rather than a single sitting.

Pass 1 — Orient (5–10 minutes)

Read only: title, abstract, introduction, every section heading, and the conclusion. Skip the whole middle.

Then, before going further, answer in one sentence each:

  • What is this about?
  • What does the author want me to believe by the end?

If you cannot answer these yet, read the introduction again. Authors almost always state their claim in the last paragraph or two of the introduction — look for phrases like “I argue that,” “this article shows,” or “the central claim is.”

Pass 2 — Read for the meat of the argument or conceptual contribution (time depends on length and complexity)

Now read it through. But read like you are following an argument, not collecting facts.

  • Read the first sentence of each paragraph. Academic paragraphs are usually built so the first sentence carries the point. If that sentence tells you what you need, move on.
  • Locate the “because.” Every claim should have a “because” to defend it. Find and mark the because. If the “because” is missing, that’s a weakness that you should note.
  • Let go of the details you do not need. You are allowed to skim a statistical table, a long literature review, or three pages of an example you already understand. Skimming on purpose is a skill, not a failure. Just don’t overdo it, especially when you are reading material on a topic that is completely new to you.
  • Look up unfamiliar terms sparingly. Any more and you are researching, not reading. Guess from context and keep moving; often the author defines it two pages later anyway. Keep a list of unfamiliar terms that you can look up later or ask me in class to define.

Pass 3 — Interrogate (5–10 minutes)

Close the reading. Then ask:

  • Do I actually buy this? What would have to be true for it to hold?
  • What is the strongest objection someone could raise?
  • What does this let me see that I could not see before?
  • Where does it connect to something else in this course?

Pass 3 is where the reading becomes your tool for analytic discovery rather than a random thing you have happened to have read. In a seminar of four people there is nowhere to hide, and the difference between a session that works and one that does not is whether people arrive having done Pass 3. It is also where the material becomes usable later — in your précis, in your final project, and in the arguments you will make in your own professional writing after this degree.

The same three passes, applied to a book

Books are where most of your semester goes, and they differ from articles in three ways that matter for method.

The order of the chapters is informative about the structure of the argument. In an article, the section order is largely conventional and tells you very little. In a book, the author has decided what has to be established before what else can be argued, and an author who puts the historical material in chapter one is claiming something different than an author who puts the same material in an appendix. You can treat the table of contents as an outline of the argument, because that is more or less what it is.

Chapters have different jobs, and they are not equally important. Some state the argument. Some supply evidence for a claim already made. Some anticipate objections. Some extend the argument to a case the author found interesting but that the argument does not actually depend on. Reading all of them at the same intensity ends up preventing you from identifying the central claims and contributions of the book.

You will almost never be assigned a whole book at once. Seminars break books into halves or thirds across two or three sessions, as this one does. That means the argument arrives in installments, the material that would correct your first impression is often assigned for a subsequent class session, and the chapters in front of you at any given moment are the ones that make least sense standing alone, because middle chapters are written for readers who already know where the book is going. Students who treat each chunk as a self-contained assignment finish the term with three sets of chapter notes per book and no account of what any of the books argued.

The three passes still apply. They just get distributed across the sessions the book occupies.

Pass 1 — Orient, before the first session on the book (60–90 minutes)

Do all of this before the first assigned chunk, not spread out over the weeks the book occupies. This is the session that takes the most time up front, but it makes reading easier as you move along.

  • The apparatus, first (15–20 minutes). Main titles are often chosen by publishers to be memorable, but subtitles usually have to state the substance — Embedded Autonomy: States and Industrial Transformation names both the thing being explained and the thing doing the explaining. Then the table of contents: note which chapters look theoretical, which look empirical, and guess at each chapter’s job. Then the preface or acknowledgments, which are worth reading for practical rather than sentimental reasons, since they tell you when the research was actually done, where the fieldwork or data came from, and which scholarly community the author is writing within. Then the index, briefly, scanning for the concepts and cases with the longest page runs; those are usually the book’s real preoccupations, which do not always match what the chapter titles advertise.
  • The introduction, completely and slowly. This is the highest-yield reading you will do in the entire book, and where you should be most reluctant to skim. A well-made introduction contains the puzzle, the argument, the alternatives being rejected, the evidence base, the scope conditions, and a chapter-by-chapter roadmap. Take your notes now, not at the end.
  • The conclusion, even before it is assigned. This is the step people skip, and when a book is assigned in pieces it is the one that matters most. The conclusion tells you what the author believes was established by the end, which is not always the same as what the introduction promised, and where those two diverge you often have the most interesting thing about the book. More practically, it gives you the argument’s ending position before the middle starts arriving in installments, so you have a better idea of what analytic task each chapter is performing. Academic books are not built to reward suspense and knowing the destination will help you make smart choices about where to focus your attention for the middle sections of the book.

Pass 2 — Read for the argument, one chunk per session

Everything in Pass 2 above still holds: first sentences, locate the “because,” skim on purpose. Four additions for books.

  • Reread your own statement of the argument before you start. This is what keeps the installments connected to each other, and skipping it is how people end up reading chapters five through seven as though the book had started there.
  • Read each chapter at the intensity its analytic job deserves. Theory or framework chapters get close reading, since this is where concepts are defined and the mechanism is specified, and the definitions are what you will actually use later. Empirical and case chapters get a structured pass instead of a linear one: read the first two or three pages (what this case is supposed to show) and the last two or three (what the author says it showed), then move through the middle looking for how the evidence is being used rather than for the narrative. You need to be able to say what a case does for the argument. You do not need to be able to recount the case. You can read the case in greater depth if that particular case is of special interest to you or if you are looking for examples of how to structure a case write up.
  • Answer one question about the chunk as a whole. What did these chapters do to the argument you already had? The useful answers are few: they confirmed it with evidence, extended it to a case you would not have expected it to cover, qualified it by adding a scope condition, complicated it by introducing something the introduction did not prepare you for, or failed to support it. Write down which, in a sentence, and revise your one-sentence statement of the argument while keeping the earlier version. How that sentence changes across sessions is a useful diagnostic: if it never changes you may be reading passively, and if it changes substantially then something in the middle chapters is doing more work than the introduction let on, which is worth raising in class.
  • Summarize the key points in each chapter and the key analytic purpose of that chapter (that is, what job it is performing in the overall structure of the book’s argument).

Pass 3 — Interrogate, after the last session on the book (15 minutes)

Same four questions as before, plus: name the chapter you would have to reread to argue with the author seriously, and name the other book or article on the syllabus this one most directly disagrees with.

Do this while the book is still fresh in your mind rather than in December. This reassembly is what you will actually use in the précis and the final project, and reconstructing it from chunk notes two months later takes considerably longer than fifteen minutes.

Triage: what to protect when you are short on time

Within a chapter, in order of value: first two pages and last two pages; all section headings in sequence; the first sentence of each paragraph in the sections you have not yet understood; anything the author sets off typographically, since authors do not box material they consider incidental; then the full text of the passages where the argument is most directly articulated. Skip footnotes on a first pass unless they are substantive rather than citational — though in many fields the substantive footnotes are where the author has parked a potential objection they could not fully answer, so you should read these on a second pass if you have time.

Across the reading schedule, protect the first day of reading. If you are going to cut corners somewhere, cut them in the second or third chunk of a book rather than the first. Cutting a middle chunk costs you a chapter’s worth of evidence, which you can generally recover in discussion. Cutting the orientation compromises everything you read afterward, because you spend the remaining sessions accumulating chapters with no analytic or argument structure to fit them into.

Signals worth training yourself to notice

Concessions. To be sure. Critics might object. This is not to say. Admittedly. Each of these marks a place where the author is managing a weakness in the argument. You will usually find the scope conditions attached to a concession, and a concession is the most efficient starting point for a serious objection.

Hedges that shift between chapters. An argument stated as causes in the introduction and as is associated with by chapter five indicates that the scholar has softened their causal claim over time — usually because the evidence is weak.

What the author defends versus what they assume/take for granted. It is often hard, early in a graduate program, to tell which parts of a book are the author’s own contribution and which are simply standard in the field. The most reliable signal is proportionality: claims the author treats as settled get a citation and a subordinate clause, while claims original to the author need a much longer explanation. Whatever is defended at length is the contribution. This is also the fastest way to work out what a field takes for granted, which is worth knowing for its own sake.

The cases that are not there. If a book on industrial policy in East Asia never takes up the failures, or a book on institutions covers five countries that all happen to fit, that is worth noticing. Which cases an author selects is part of the argument, and authors rarely defend the selection explicitly. (If they do have a case selection section, be sure to read that closely and think about whether their case design is appropriate.)

Know what kind of material you are reading, and adjust accordingly

“Scholarly work” covers several very different genres. The elements above generalize, but where you find the answers changes depending on genre. Figure out the genre in Pass 1 and you will stop looking for things in the wrong places.

Articles and shorter work

Genre How to recognize it Argument location On what to take notes
Descriptive empirical / survey Reports what a body of research has found; lots of citations to other studies, few or no original tables Intro and conclusion — the author’s claim is about the state of knowledge The pattern in the findings, the conditions under which it holds, what is still unsettled
Hypothesis-testing empirical Has original data, a method section, results tables, a stated hypothesis Intro states the finding; results section tests it The hypothesis, how the concepts were measured, what result would have falsified it
Theory / conceptual article No data; defines and distinguishes concepts Distributed across the piece; the conclusion pulls it together The definitions, the distinctions, the mechanism connecting them
Review essay Discusses several other people’s books or articles The author’s own take on how the works relate — often a framework of their own Each position, and what the reviewer thinks each gets right or wrong
Policy report Institutional author (UN, World Bank, think tank), executive summary Executive summary and recommendations The recommendation and the interest behind it
Working paper NBER, IMF, or World Bank series number; no journal name Same as the equivalent published genre Same, plus: this has not been peer reviewed. Check whether a published version exists, and note the date

Books

Genre How to recognize it Argument location On what to take notes
Theoretical monograph Few or no original data; the work is conceptual — defining, distinguishing, reframing Distributed; the introduction states it, later chapters develop and qualify it The definitions, the distinctions, and the relationships the author claims between them
Theory-plus-evidence monograph A framework chapter followed by cases or data chapters Introduction and framework chapter; the empirical chapters test or illustrate The mechanism, and what each case is doing — confirming, extending, or handling a hard test
Synthesis or state-of-the-field book Organized around competing explanations rather than a single thesis The assessment itself is the contribution; look for the author’s verdict on each explanation Each explanation, the evidence for and against it, and the author’s basis for adjudicating
Revisionist or corrective book Frames itself against a dominant view, often in the first paragraph The target and the correction; the empirical chapters are mostly counterevidence What exactly is being corrected, and whether the correction is a rejection or a qualification
Trade-crossover research book Written for a general audience by researchers; short chapters, anecdote-led, minimal apparatus Often methodological as much as substantive — a claim about how we should know things The underlying studies and what they establish; separate the finding from the storytelling
Edited volume Multiple authors, thematic chapters In the editors’ introduction, which is a real argument and not a summary The chapter you were assigned, plus the framing the editors put around it

Three genre traps to remember

“This author just keeps describing other people’s work.” You may be holding a review essay, a literature survey, or, at book length, a synthesis or state-of-the-field book. The author is not confused and has not become lost in their writing — mapping the debate is the contribution. Read for the map of positions rather than for a single buried thesis, and watch for the analytic move where the author uses other people’s work to build a framework of their own; that framework is usually the real payoff. In a synthesis book, what you want is the basis on which the author decides that one explanation survives contact with the evidence and another does not.

“There is data here but no hypothesis.” Not all empirical work tests a hypothesis. Descriptive empirical work establishes what is true — patterns, magnitudes, conditions — and that is a legitimate and demanding contribution in its own right. Do not go hunting for a hypothesis test that was never promised.

“The author spends three hundred pages attacking a position I have never encountered.” That is a revisionist or corrective book, and you are missing the target rather than the argument. Reconstruct the target first — the introduction almost always names it, sometimes in the opening paragraph — because a correction cannot be evaluated without understanding what is being argued against. Then ask the question that decides what kind of book it is: does the author want the dominant view abandoned, or qualified? Those are very different claims, and authors are not always as clear about which one they are making as they could be.

Reading technical work you cannot fully evaluate

You will be assigned papers with formal models or econometrics beyond what you can independently verify. This is normal and it is not a reason to skip them or to read only the abstract.

  • Treat the assumptions as the argument. In a formal model, nearly all of the interesting intellectual content is in the setup — what the agents want, what they know, what constrains them, what is taken as given from outside the model. If you understand the assumptions, you can evaluate whether the paper is relevant to a question you care about even when you cannot follow the derivation.
  • Find where the model is stated in words. Good theory papers state each result verbally somewhere near the formal statement of it. Those sentences are what you need; the mathematics is the demonstration that they follow from the assumptions.
  • For empirical work, ask what would have falsified this. You do not need to assess an identification strategy in technical detail to ask what the comparison is, what variation the author is exploiting, and what else could have produced the same pattern.
  • Look at the figures. In empirical economics the main figure usually contains the finding. If you understand one figure and the paragraph interpreting it, you have the paper’s core.
  • Note what the result is actually about. A precisely estimated effect on a narrow outcome in one setting is a different kind of claim than a broad statement about development, and papers are often summarized as the latter when they establish the former.

A note-taking template that works for all scholarly writing

Use this for every reading. For an article it might only be half a page, handwritten or typed, and should take five minutes after Pass 2. Save this material in your course folder; you will use it again.

For a book, keep one running document, opened when the book is first assigned and revised after every session on it — not a fresh set of notes each week. Because the books are assigned in pieces, these notes are where the pieces get reassembled. Fill in as much as you can during Pass 1 from the introduction and conclusion, then correct it as the chapters arrive.

Citation: author, year, short title

Genre: which row of the tables above

Question: what is the author trying to explain or settle?

Claim: their answer, in your words — one or two sentences, no quoting

Mechanism or evidence: why is the claim supposed to hold? What is doing the work?

Key concepts: the two or three terms you would need to use this reading in an argument

Scope and limits: when would this stop being true?

My pushback: one objection, question, or thing that did not convince you

Course link: which reading, concept, or session does this speak to?

For books, add three lines:

Target: what view or literature is this arguing against, and does the author want it abandoned or qualified?

Chapter map: one line per chapter — what job it does, and whether the argument depends on it

Chunk log: one line per session — what those chapters did to the argument (confirmed / extended / qualified / complicated / failed to support), and your revised one-sentence statement, with the earlier version kept above it

Two extremely important rules to increase effectiveness:

Write the claim in your own words. If you can only copy the author’s sentence, you have not understood it yet. This also means not using AI, because your notes are not summaries, they are analysis. What makes the exercise worth doing is the work of compressing three hundred pages into two sentences, and a compression performed by something other than you leaves you with a document you cannot use and an understanding you do not have.

Always fill in “my pushback.” Even a weak objection forces you into an active stance toward the text. “I did not understand why X follows from Y” is a perfectly good entry — and it is often the best thing you can bring to class.

Annotating without drowning in yellow

If you highlight everything, you have highlighted nothing. Try marking in the margin instead, with a small code:

  • C — the central claim
  • M — the mechanism, or a step in it
  • ? — I do not follow this
  • ! — this surprised me or I disagree
  • X — a concession or scope condition
  • → — connects to something else we read
  • def — a definition I will need later

The point of margin marks is that you can find your way back.

When you get lost

You will get lost. Everyone does, including me (especially if the writing is poor). What separates strong readers is how they adjust:

  • Keep going. Confusion in the middle often resolves by the conclusion, or by the end of the chapter. Read to the end before you decide you are stuck.
  • Go back to the introduction. Nine times out of ten, the thing you lost was the thread of the argument, and the intro is where the argument is most clearly articulated. For a book, this means the book’s introduction, not the chapter’s.
  • Name the confusion specifically. “I do not get this book” is not usable. “I do not see how his definition of embedded autonomy differs from ordinary state capacity” is a question you can bring to class — and it is usually a question several other people also have.
  • Do not assume the fault is yours. Sometimes an argument really is underspecified, a concept is doing work it cannot bear, or a chapter is padding. Noticing that is an important skill, and saying it out loud with evidence is a good part of what a seminar is for.
  • Read reviews after you have formed a view, not before. Published reviews in field journals are useful for locating a book in its debate, and actively unhelpful if you read them first, because you will then be defending someone else’s reaction rather than developing your own.

How much is enough?

Because you will spend most of the term partway through a book, there are three versions of this question.

For an article, you have read well enough when you can, without looking at the page:

  • State the author’s claim in one sentence
  • Say why they think it is true
  • Name one thing that would make you doubt it
  • Say what it has to do with this course

Before a session in the middle of a book, you are ready when you can:

  • State the book’s argument in a sentence, as you currently understand it
  • Say what this chunk’s chapters were doing for that argument
  • Say whether they changed your statement of it, and how
  • Name one thing in these chapters you could not make fit

That last item does not mean that you have failed as a reader. A chapter you cannot fit into the argument is either a signal that your statement of the argument needs revising or a genuine weakness in the book. Bring this to class so we can figure it out together!

Once you have finished a book, you have read it well enough when you can:

  • State the argument in two sentences
  • Name what it is arguing against, and why the author thinks the target is wrong
  • Describe the evidence base and one thing it cannot show
  • Name the chapter you would have to reread to argue with the author seriously
  • Connect it to at least one other thing on the syllabus

If you can do these things, you are prepared, regardless of how many pages you skimmed to get there. If you cannot, more time spent reading linearly will not help — go back to the introduction and the conclusion and work outward again.

Reading in this course specifically

This course is built almost entirely on books. Six of them, read in chunks of three or four chapters over two or three sessions each, plus a set of articles and working papers in the second half of the term. Most sessions open with a short slide-based lecture to anchor the day, but the bulk of the hour is discussion among four people, so the reading does more than prepare you for the session. It supplies most of the material the session has to work with.

The Schedule has the chapter assignments, the Session Notes & Discussion Roles page has the note requirements and the rotation, and the Course Précis page explains the assignment that most of your reading notes eventually feed.

The six books and what kind of book each one is

Book Genre What to look for
Sen, Development as Freedom Theoretical monograph The conceptual moves — what freedom is doing as both the means and the end of development, and how capability differs from income, utility, and resources
Koyama & Rubin, How the World Became Rich Synthesis / state-of-the-field Each competing explanation of sustained growth, the evidence for and against it, and the authors’ basis for adjudicating between them

Verify these against the books themselves as you go. If your assessment of a book’s genre differs from mine, that disagreement is worth raising in class — it usually means we are reading the argument differently, which is a productive place to start a session.

Where the reading load actually falls

Sen comes in halves across two sessions; the other five come in thirds across three. The practical consequence is that the load in this course is uneven by design, and it is worth planning around rather than being surprised by. The first session on each book is the one that should take the most time to read, because that is when you do Pass 1 on the whole book plus the first chunk’s chapters. Budget something closer to half again the usual time for those six sessions. The second and third sessions on the same book should then be lighter than the page count suggests, because by then you know the argument, you know which chapters the conclusion leans on, and you can read a case chapter for what it contributes rather than for its contents.

Session notes and discussion roles

The session notes are where your reading becomes visible to everyone else. They are your own words, without AI assistance, for the reason given above: the compression is the skill being assessed, and a summary you did not produce yourself is a summary you can’t fully defend.

Part 1 of the note asks for the claim, the evidence, and one point you are prepared to argue. Those map onto the template above: the claim line, stated as what causes what, through what mechanism; the mechanism or evidence line, with the kind of evidence named; and the my pushback line. If you have been keeping a running document on the book, most of Part 1 is already drafted before you sit down to write it.

Part 2 depends on which of the four roles you hold that week. The role does not change the method, but it changes what you prioritize while reading:

  • Discussion Leader — you provide the seminar three to five questions to ground discussion. The most productive places to find useful questions are the concessions, where the author is managing a weakness; the seams between chapters, where evidence presented in one chapter is supposed to support a claim made in another; and, in a session partway through a book, the gap between what the introduction promised and what these particular chapters have delivered so far. Questions drawn from those places tend to open up discussion, whereas questions drawn from the introduction tend to produce a round of summary that everyone in the room could already have given. (But, we do need to spend some time in class summarizing what the book is about and the argument it is making.)
  • Skeptic — read for the weakest link in the causal chain. The signals section above is written for you: track the concessions, watch for hedges that soften between chapters, and pay attention to which cases are missing. Ask what variation the author is exploiting and what the evidence cannot reach even if the argument is correct.
  • Connector — the course-link line of your notes is most of your preparation, and it is worth filling in every week rather than only in the weeks you hold this role. Look particularly for places where the books contradict each other. This syllabus deliberately assigns books that disagree, and working out the terms of the disagreement is a good deal of what we are doing this semester. Your connections also feed the shared causal map, which is the reason to keep this material rather than discard it after class.
  • Practitioner — read the mechanism with an eye to what it would take to act on it. When you reach a mechanism in Pass 2, ask who would have to do what, what it would cost, and who would resist. The scope and limits line of your notes matters most here, since an argument’s scope conditions are usually what determine whether a policy implication survives contact with a real country.

A note on the reading load and the rotation: roles rotate weekly and you hold each one for both sessions of a week, so your role sometimes lands on the orientation session of a new book and sometimes in the middle of one. Doing Pass 1 on the whole book at the start is what makes the middle-of-book weeks manageable in whatever role you are holding.

The articles and working papers

The second half of the term adds journal articles, an edited-volume chapter, and several NBER working papers, some of them formal or technical. Use the three passes in their original single-sitting form; these are single-session readings and the compressed method is sufficient. For the technical papers, the guidance above applies directly: read the assumptions as the argument, read the verbal statements of the results as the results, and do not expect to independently verify a derivation. You are expected to be able to say what the paper claims, what would have to be true for the claim to hold, and whether the result is as broad as the abstract implies.

If you are behind

You will occasionally arrive at a session having read less than you intended. Handle it by triage rather than by silence.

If it is the first session on a book, protect Pass 1 and sacrifice the chapters: the apparatus, the introduction, and the conclusion, then the first and last pages of each assigned chapter. You will be able to discuss the argument even without much of the evidence, and you will not have damaged the remaining sessions on that book.

If it is a later session, protect the chapters and lean on the notes you already have: reread your statement of the argument, then read one of the assigned chapters properly and take the others at first-and-last-pages.

Either way, say what you did. “I read chapter five closely and only skimmed six and seven” is a legitimate contribution, and it helps us understand if claims you are making about chapter six rest on close reading or a sketchy scan of the material. The worse position to be in is having moved evenly across all of the pages without retaining an argument, which is the outcome that the front-to-back habit produces when the assignment gets long enough.

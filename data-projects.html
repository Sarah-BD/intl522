Data Projects

Two applied exercises in reading development data · 7.5% each

You will complete two data projects during this semester — one at the macro and one at the micro level.

In the first, you look at development from above — the aggregate measures that governments, international organizations, and scholars use to say whether a country is developing.

In the second, you look from below — household-level data on how poor families actually earn, spend, borrow, and manage risk.

These projects, then, function in part to help us address a question of levels in development. How do individual behaviors aggregate to economy-wide progress on a range of development measures? Is development an individualistic/household project or a social one?

These are data literacy projects, not statistics projects. I am not asking you to run regressions, and you do not need any statistical training beyond careful reading of charts and tables. The skills being practiced here are the ones you will use constantly in policy careers: finding credible data, understanding what a measure actually captures, presenting numbers in an unbiased way, and drawing conclusions that the data can support.

AI use on these projects

Per the course AI policy, you may use generative AI to help you find data, write chart code, and wrangle spreadsheets — with disclosure. The selection of measures, the analysis, and every interpretive claim must be your own, and you will present and defend the work in class. Include a brief AI-use note with your submission. You also must double check that your AI agent is providing you with real data rather than hallucinations. In class, we will briefly discuss strategies for verification when using AI tools.

Data Project 1 — Measuring Development

Assigned Mon Aug 31 · Due Friday Sep 4 by COB, on Canvas · 7.5%

How development is measured shapes what development means — this is one of Sen’s central points, and this project helps you see this in practice by comparing different measures of development.

The task

  1. Choose a region (Sub-Saharan Africa, South Asia, Latin America, Southeast Asia, MENA, or Central Asia), and within it, three focus countries with different development trajectories. Each student should take a different region — coordinate on the sign-up sheet posted on Canvas.
  2. Choose at least three distinct measures of development. At least one must be income-based (GDP per capita, ideally PPP-adjusted; or the international poverty headcount) and at least one must be non-income or composite (HDI, the Multidimensional Poverty Index, inequality-adjusted HDI, life expectancy, mean years of schooling). The resources page lists where you can find each of these data sources.
  3. Chart the trends. For each measure, show how your focus countries have moved over the longest sensible window the data allow (for GDP per capita that may be 60 years; for the MPI it may be 15 — and that difference in coverage is itself worth discussing). Every figure needs labeled axes, units, years, and a source line.
  4. Analyze what the measures capture and hide. Where do your measures agree about which country is doing well, and where do they disagree? What does each measure assume development is? What would Sen say about each? Which countries look different depending on the yardstick — and why, concretely?
  5. Take a position. Close with a short recommendation: if you could report only one of your measures to a minister or country director to track this region’s progress, which would you choose, and what would you be knowingly giving up?

The deliverable

A data brief of 3–4 pages (single-spaced, figures included in the page count), written for an intelligent professional reader, plus a data appendix (not counted in the page limit) listing every series you used: indicator name, source, years covered, and any gaps or oddities you encountered. Submit as a PDF on Canvas by COB Sept 4, and come to class on Sept 9 ready to present your findings in about five minutes at the debrief — expect questions about why the measures disagree.

Data Project 2 — The Economic Lives of the Poor

Assigned week of Nov 16 · Due Fri Nov 20, COB; on Canvas · presented at the Nov 30 debrief · 7.5%

Banerjee and Duflo’s Poor Economics emphasizes a different level of analysis: instead of analyzing development as a country-level attribute, they are concerned with household development outcomes. This project has you explore that micro level with real household-survey data, and then compare what you find with the macro picture you built in Project 1.

As many authors we’ve read this semester have noted, national-level averages often obscure important variation within countries — within income deciles but also across subnational units and/or demographic groups. In this project you will explore within-country variation in development outcomes so that you can develop a more grounded understanding of how much country averages hide.

The task

  1. Choose one country. Ideally, use one of your three focus countries from Project 1 so the macro–micro comparison is direct. Before you commit, check the coverage table in the quick-start guide below (the quick-start guide was developed with AI assistance, so you should double-check for accuracy). If your first-choice country has no recent Demographic and Health Survey data, it is probably best to switch to a country in the same region that does.

  2. Use two required household-survey sources. Both data sources below publish ready-made tables broken out by a household’s place in the country-wide distribution, so you do not need to clean raw microdata. If you find yourself spending time on data cleaning, please STOP. That means something has gone wrong. Come see me instead.

    • DHS (Demographic and Health Surveys), via STATcompiler or the direct-download link in the quick-start guide below. Results are broken out by wealth quintile (poorest 20% to richest 20% of households, ranked by an index of assets), urban/rural residence, and region.
    • Global Findex 2025 (survey fielded in 2024). Results are broken out by income group (the poorest 40% and the richest 60% of households within the country), urban/rural residence, and sex.

  3. Build a portrait of poor households’ economic lives that covers three of the five domains listed below, using at least six indicators in total (so, two indicators per domain). For every indicator, show the poorest group alongside the richest group and the national average.

    • Earning a living: agricultural vs. non-farm work, wage income, payment in cash or in kind, income from crop sales
    • Financial lives: accounts and mobile money, saving (formal and informal), borrowing (from family, savings groups, shops, lenders)
    • Resilience to adversity: coming up with emergency money, where it comes from, how long a household could last without its main income, health insurance, difficulty paying for medical care, exposure to natural disasters
    • Investing in the future: school attendance at primary and secondary age, child stunting, worry about school fees, saving for old age
    • Living conditions: electricity, water, sanitation, land and housing ownership, compared across urban/rural or regions (see the warning about wealth quintiles in the guide)

  4. Check your measures against each other. Your two sources define “the poor” differently: DHS ranks households by the assets they own, while Findex ranks them by the income respondents report. Find at least one place where the two sources ostensibly measure the same concept (account ownership and mobile money are the easiest) and explain any disagreement between them. Is it the definition of the poor, who was asked (all adults, or only women aged 15–49), the denominator, the survey year, or the wording of the question? Where DHS reports a confidence interval, use it.

  5. Compare the two levels. Where does your country’s household picture confirm what Banerjee and Duflo report, and where does it diverge from their composite picture of “the poor”? Most importantly: what does the micro view show that your Project 1 aggregates concealed? And is there anything the aggregates showed that the household data cannot?

The deliverable

Same format as Project 1: a 3–4 page data brief (single-spaced, figures included) and a data appendix, submitted as a PDF on Canvas by Friday, Nov 20 at COB, plus a roughly five-minute presentation at the Nov 30 debrief.

For each series, the data appendix lists: the indicator name exactly as the source labels it, source, survey year, how the poor group is defined (wealth quintile, income group, or residence), who is in the denominator (for example, “women 15–49 who own a mobile phone”), and the confidence interval if one is reported.

How I will evaluate both projects

Criterion Weight What I am looking for
Data handling & documentation 25% Appropriate sources; correct units and comparisons; a complete, honest data appendix; gaps acknowledged rather than papered over
Figures 25% Charts that are accurate, labeled, sourced, and readable — a figure should make its point without the caption doing all the work
Interpretation & measurement critique 30% Analysis that goes beyond describing the lines: what the measures assume, what they hide, where they disagree, and why
Connection & communication 20% Explicit engagement with course arguments (Sen for Project 1; Banerjee & Duflo for Project 2); clear, precise prose within the page limit

The most common way to lose points on assignments like these is to submit a data dump — many charts, little argument. The second most common way is false precision: treating a poverty rate as exact when the underlying survey has a confidence interval and a five-year gap between rounds. Noticing imprecision is not a weakness of your brief; it is an important skill to develop.

Step 1: DHS tables

Route 1: STATcompiler (point and click). Go to statcompiler.com and choose your country, then your indicators. Add wealth quintile and residence as background characteristics, and export to Excel.

Route 2: one-click download (most reliable). Paste this address into your browser, replacing TZ with your country’s two-letter DHS code and 2022 with your survey year. The browser will display a CSV, which you save with File → Save. DHS codes do not always match ISO codes: India is IA, Nepal is NP, Bangladesh is BD, and Cambodia is KH. The full list is at api.dhsprogram.com/rest/dhs/countries.

https://api.dhsprogram.com/rest/dhs/data?countryIds=TZ&surveyYear=2022&breakdown=all&perpage=5000&f=csv&indicatorIds=EM_OCCP_W_AGR,EM_OCCP_M_AGR,EM_WERN_W_WIF,CO_MOBB_W_BNK,CO_MOBB_M_BNK,CO_MOBB_W_MBF,CO_MOBB_M_MBF,AH_HINS_W_ANY,AH_HINS_M_ANY,RH_PAHC_W_MON,ED_NARP_B_BTH,ED_NARS_B_BTH,CN_NUTS_C_HA2,WE_OWNA_W_LNO,HC_ELEC_H_ELC,ED_LITR_W_LIT

This link pulls 16 indicators: work in agriculture (women, men); women’s control over their own earnings; bank account and mobile-money use (women, men); any health insurance (women, men); “getting money for treatment” as a barrier to health care; net primary and secondary school attendance; child stunting; women who own no land; household electricity; and women’s literacy. Each indicator comes with every breakdown the survey publishes. Filter the CharacteristicCategory column to Wealth quintile, Residence, or Total. Read the Value, CILow, and CIHigh columns, and use DenominatorWeighted to see how many people a number rests on.

Two things to watch in DHS.

  • Denominators. Many DHS indicators cover a subgroup: women aged 15–49, employed women, or women who own a mobile phone. Read the indicator definition before you compare it with anything else.
  • Circularity. The DHS wealth quintiles are built from the assets a household owns, including electricity, water source, and toilet type. “Households with electricity by wealth quintile” is therefore partly true by construction. For living conditions, compare urban with rural or one region with another.

Step 2: Global Findex 2025

  • Excel or CSV: On the Global Findex download page, take the country-level file (GlobalFindexDatabase2025.xlsx). It reports each indicator for all adults and separately for the poorest 40% and richest 60%, rural and urban adults, and women and men. The glossary file on the same page explains each indicator code.
  • Browse online: The same data are in the World Bank’s Data360 Findex dataset and in DataBank.

Good indicators for this project include: account ownership (financial institution vs. mobile money); saved any money; saved using a savings club; borrowed from family or friends; purchased food on credit; borrowed for health purposes; coming up with emergency funds in 30 days (and its main source: family, work, sale of assets); how long the household could cover expenses after losing its main income; experienced a natural disaster; most worrying financial issue (medical costs, school fees); received wages; and received payments for agricultural products.

Watch: Some Findex indicators are shares of all adults and others are shares of account owners only. If a “total” falls outside the range of its own subgroups, the denominators differ.

Optional third source

  • FAO Rural Livelihoods (RuLIS), on FAOSTAT (fao.org/faostat/en/#data/RLIS). These are harmonized indicators from about 50 countries’ household income surveys, many of them World Bank LSMS. This is the best free source for where household income comes from: the share of income from farming, agricultural wages, non-farm work, and transfers, and the share of crops eaten rather than sold. Results are broken out by rural/urban and by male- vs. female-headed household, not by quintile. On the FAOSTAT page, select a survey, indicators, and a qualifier; under “Source,” click Select All, or the query returns nothing. Coverage varies by survey: some survey years publish only the urban or only the rural figures.

Look at the two mobile-money rows. DHS seems to show far more mobile-money use than Findex, and a much steeper gap between rich and poor. Neither source is simply wrong. DHS compares the poorest 20% with the richest 20% and counts only women who own a phone. Findex compares the poorest 40% with the richest 60% and counts all adults. And notice that borrowing barely varies with income at all, which is a very Banerjee-and-Duflo finding.
